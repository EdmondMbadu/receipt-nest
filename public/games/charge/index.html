What is this charge on my bank statement?

Your statement isn’t hiding anything. It’s just short on space.

A charge on your statement is built from a few pieces: the company that processed the card payment, a shortened version of the business name, and sometimes a store number, a city, or words your bank adds. That’s why a coffee shop can show up as SQ *BLUE DOOR COFFE.

  • Payment company prefixes. Many small businesses take cards through a payment company, and its code comes first: SQ * for Square, TST* for Toast.
  • A character limit. The name on a statement is usually limited to about 22 characters, so long names get cut off or lose their vowels: GRNLINE PLMB SUP.
  • Numbers and places. Chains add store numbers (#6512), and in-person charges often end with a city and state.
  • Bank wording. Some banks add their own words, like POS DEBIT for a card used at a register, or PURCHASE AUTHORIZED ON followed by the day you paid.

How to identify a charge you don’t recognize

  1. Match the amount and date against your receipts and email receipts. This solves most mystery charges in seconds, if you kept them.
  2. Read the prefix to learn which payment company processed it, then look at what follows for the business name.
  3. Use the company’s lookup. Square offers a way to look up a payment if you don’t have the receipt, Shopify has a page for unknown charges, and Amazon lists how its charges appear.
  4. Still a mystery? Call your bank. It can tell you more about the merchant, and you can dispute a charge you didn’t make.

This is general information. If you think a charge is fraud, contact your bank right away.

Why a receipt answers what a statement can’t

A statement tells you where you paid and how much. It almost never says what you bought. SQ *PRINTWORKS COPY $28.00 could be business cards or a birthday poster, and only the receipt knows. That matters most at tax time, when the question is always “what was this for?” We dug into it in Can bank statements replace receipts for taxes?
