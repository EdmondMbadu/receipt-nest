A pile is just receipts you haven’t snapped yet

Each receipt takes a few seconds to snap the day you get it. A hundred of them at once is an afternoon.

With nobody snapping, the glass in this game usually gives out somewhere between 90 and 105 receipts, worth around $4,500 to $5,200 in our test runs. And it speeds up as it goes: the bigger the ball, the less room it has, so it hits the glass more often and grows faster.

A real backlog works the same way. So the habit that matters is the one the game asks for: snap the receipt when it lands. Here’s how to make that photo count.

How to take a receipt photo that holds up

  • Lay it flat on a dark, plain surface, in even light, with no glare across the print.
  • Get the whole receipt in the frame: the store name at the top, the date, the items and the total. A long receipt can take two photos.
  • One receipt per photo. A stack in one shot is hard to read and harder to find later.
  • Check that it’s readable before the paper goes in your pocket. Most receipts are thermal paper, and the print fades. Here’s why receipts fade.
  • Keep the itemized receipt, not just the card slip. The slip shows that you paid; the itemized receipt shows what you bought. Here’s the difference.
  • Add a few words about why. “Printer ink, client proposals” takes five seconds now and saves guessing in April.

IRS guidance on electronic records, Revenue Procedure 97-22, calls for copies with “a high degree of legibility and readability.” A sharp, complete photo is what that looks like in practice. If a particular receipt matters a lot, ask your tax professional before you throw away the original.

This is general record-keeping information, not tax advice. Your tax professional can tell you what your situation needs.

Already have a pile?

Don’t try to snap it in one sitting. Sort it into months first, then photograph one month at a time and check it against that month’s bank and card statements. The step-by-step version is how to sort a shoebox of receipts, and the routine that keeps it from coming back is how to keep track of receipts all year.

How to play The Pile in 3D

  • A ball made of receipts bounces inside a glass sphere. Every time it hits the glass, one more receipt sticks to it and the ball grows.
  • Tap anywhere, or press Space, the moment it lands. A circle closes on the glass around each landing: tap before it closes and that receipt is snapped, so the pile shrinks.
  • Tap when nothing has landed and you fumble one, which grows the pile. Mashing doesn’t work.
  • The bigger the pile, the less room it has, so it lands more often. The bounces also speed up as the year goes on.
  • Get through 100 receipts before the pile fills the glass. Snap all 100 for a perfect year.
  • Watch lets the pile grow with nobody snapping. Drag to turn the view and see it from any side.

Prefer it flat? The original The Pile uses a ring instead of a sphere.

Questions

Is every run different?

Yes. The starting throw, every bounce angle and every receipt amount are random, so no two runs are the same. In Watch mode the glass usually gives out somewhere between 90 and 105 receipts.

Is this the same game as The Pile?

Same rules, new shape. The ring became a glass sphere, and the ball can fly in any direction instead of around a flat circle. It sits on a floor and casts a shadow, so you can follow it in depth.

What is the song?

The Can-Can, from Jacques Offenbach’s Orpheus in the Underworld (1858), which is in the public domain. Every landing plays the next note, the tune moves up a key each time it comes round, and brass joins when the box is nearly full. Snapping a receipt adds a xylophone note on top.

Does it work on my phone?

Yes, in any modern browser with 3D graphics (WebGL) turned on. On slower phones it lowers its resolution and turns off the soft shadows to keep moving smoothly. If 3D isn’t available, the 2D version runs everywhere.

Once I’ve snapped a receipt, do I still need the paper?

In general, the IRS accepts records kept electronically as long as the copies are accurate, complete and readable. Revenue Procedure 97-22 sets out the standards. If a specific receipt matters a lot, ask your tax professional before you throw away the original.
